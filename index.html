Ireland's councils list derelict sites online. Four councils publish nothing.

Every council must keep a public register of derelict sites. This map brings together the registers published online, mapped entries in all, and what they show about value, age and the gaps.

That is one line from Leitrim County Council's register of derelict sites. Commons Lane in Manorhamilton was entered on 19 June 1991, a year after the law that created the registers, and it is still there. Nothing on any register in the country has been listed for longer.

Every council has to keep a list like Leitrim's. Once a site is on it, the council can order the owner to clean it up, and can charge a levy of 7% of the site's market value for every year it stays listed.

What counts as derelict is written into the Derelict Sites Act 1990:

“any land … which detracts, or is likely to detract, to a material degree from the amenity, character or appearance of land in the neighbourhood”

The Act gives three causes: structures in a “ruinous, derelict or dangerous condition”, the “neglected, unsightly or objectionable condition” of the land, or “litter, rubbish, debris or waste”. Section 3 of the Derelict Sites Act 1990

So derelict is about condition, and it isn't the same as vacant or abandoned. Vacant is about use: a well-kept empty house isn't derelict, and empty homes are dealt with separately, through Revenue's Vacant Homes Tax. Abandoned has no legal meaning. A site appears on the map above only once a council has entered it on its register.

Read the early years with care. A register lists only what is still derelict. Sites that were repaired, sold or knocked down have come off it, so the past looks emptier than it was. Only entries from before 2000 survive .

And the date is the day the council wrote the site in. That can be long after the place fell apart.

Four registers you can't see

Kerry, Clare, Galway County and Longford keep a register but don't put it online. It can only be inspected at the council's offices or asked for, so none of their sites are on the map and their areas are hatched. Together they cover much of the west coast, from Kerry to Connemara. An empty county there means the data is missing. It doesn't mean nothing is derelict.

Cavan was on this list until September 2026, when it published its register. Its sites are on the map now, at the council's own coordinates, though the register gives townlands instead of street addresses, and no values.

About the data

All 31 local authorities were checked for a published register. publish one online; the other offer inspection only. Registers that are live map layers were read directly, with the council's own coordinates. PDFs, spreadsheets and web tables were converted one council at a time, and their addresses geocoded with OpenStreetMap services.

That makes some positions more exact than others. of the mapped sites carry the council's own coordinates and were matched to a full address. were placed on their street, and only in their town, where the circle marks the town and not the building. Entries that couldn't be placed confidently are held back and not guessed.

A checker revisits every register to flag the ones that have changed. This is not an official source: check a council's own register before relying on any entry.
